The residual highway

One block on its own is weak. But stack many blocks and training suddenly becomes hard: the learning signal barely reaches the bottom. Three small tricks fix this: residual connections, layer norm and dropout. After that, the model can be made big.

Station 1 · Telephone game

Does the learning signal reach the bottom?

During training, a correction signal (the gradient) runs backwards from the output through all layers. Without a shortcut it is multiplied by a factor at every layer, like a message in the telephone game. A residual connection adds a highway: each block only adds something to the stream, and the signal can drive straight through. Set depth and factor.

Guess first: Without a shortcut, the signal passes 40 layers and each multiplies it by 0.8. How much of it arrives at the bottom?

Without residual

With residual

The maths: without residuals the gradient is a product ∏ fl, which vanishes (f < 1) or explodes (f > 1) exponentially. With xl+1 = xl + Block(xl) every derivative is 1 + Block′. If the block starts out small, the product stays close to 1. Here: Block′ = (f − 1) · 0.1.

Station 2 · Keeping order

Layer norm

After many blocks, the numbers in a word's vector can become very large or very small. Layer norm brings them back for each word separately: mean 0, spread 1. Afterwards the model may adjust with two learned numbers. Make the values wild and see what comes out.

After

y = γ · (x − mean) / √(variance + ε) + β, computed over the features of one word, not over the batch (unlike batch norm). Modern GPTs normalise before attention and feed-forward (pre-norm), not after.

Station 3 · Don't memorise

Dropout

A large model can memorise its training text. Then the loss keeps falling on the training text but no longer on new text. Dropout switches off a random part of the neurons at every training step, so the model cannot rely on any single one.

The curves are schematic. When the finished model is used, dropout is off.
